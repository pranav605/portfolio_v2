## The Problem

One of the best parts about being a developer is curiosity. You constantly want to learn how stuff works. So you end up putting your fingers into all kinds of things just to understand them better. In the process, you learn a lot and build a ton of cool stuff.

And most importantly, you have fun. That is the entire reason you started doing it in the first place.

However, times are changing. Post-COVID, some weird shift happened in the way people think. Maybe everyone suddenly realized that we do not have much time on this space rock we live on, so now everything needs to be productive, optimized, and monetized.

Hobbyists slowly became hustlers.

People started turning every interest into a side hustle, building multiple pillars of income, and constantly trying to squeeze money out of every skill they have. Somewhere along the way, we started losing track of the most important part: having fun.

Remember when you used to spend hours designing a completely useless but pretty button with a cool animation just for the heck of it?

Apparently now you need to find a way to sell it to someone.

## The Guilt

Just to be clear, I am not against hustle culture. In some ways, it is exciting. If I am not wrong, that mindset is how many of the biggest software companies started.

But making something to sell is not the same as selling something you made.

At least in the software world, when you sit down to build something, it should be something you genuinely want to build. Something you value. Something that sounds fun to make. You should enjoy the process.

It should not start with:
"Okay, I need to print money. Let me build some random app and force people to use it."

There is nothing wrong with making money from something you built. The problem starts when every single thing you make is expected to have monetary value attached to it.

The other day I was building an app called Pingwin. It is basically a Pushover clone. I hosted a career chatbot on my portfolio website, and it used Pushover to notify me whenever someone asked a question the AI could not answer. But Pushover only gives a limited number of free notifications and I am broke, so I decided to build my own version.

I spent some time on it and ended up making a pretty solid MVP.

But instead of feeling accomplished, I felt guilty.

I kept thinking about all the time and effort I invested into something that was not making money or helping my career directly.

Definitely blaming the Instagram algorithm for this one.

Thanks for constantly showing me developers making six side hustles a week and making it feel like the entire world is building only to sell.

## The AI Effect

Speaking of Instagram, the number of influencers online has exploded over the last few years.

Before COVID, most developer creators posted tutorials, experiments, or breakdowns showing how they built things. But now, with AI becoming so accessible, almost anyone can whip up a working prototype or a fully functional vibe-coded app in a matter of hours.

And honestly, that combination of AI and hustle culture is what concerns me the most.

The amount of content coming from this crowd is unbelievable. Somehow everyone seems to be making insane amounts of money, quitting their jobs, and launching another startup every weekend.

And of course, all their secrets are one comment away.

Just comment "AI".

I do not know who started this "comment to get the roadmap" trend, but I would like to meet them offline.

Just for a polite conversation, obviously.

For me, the core idea of software engineering has always been solving real-world problems using complicated calculators called computers. Learning how they work and how to communicate with them is what made computer science exciting in the first place.

Now AI is changing that.

Programming languages are slowly becoming less important, while natural language is becoming the new interface. English is starting to feel like the programming language, and AI is becoming the translator.

That is both amazing and slightly terrifying.

We can now solve problems we never imagined could be solved, and people who never thought they could build software suddenly can.

But at the same time, there is also a lot of slop, and that is all I want to say about that.

People are no longer asking:
"Is this interesting?"

Instead, everyone is asking:
"Is this scalable?"

That shift is concerning.

## The Example

Let me give a weird example.

For the last few days, I have been seeing a fictional character in the patterns of my hardwood flooring. Yes, I know that sounds insane, but hear me out.

I even took a picture.

Somehow my brain connected a bunch of random messy lines into what looked like a full-blown anime character. The level of detail was uncanny. I could see facial features, weapons, and even a little pet standing beside the character.

I used to draw a lot, but somewhere along the way that hobby faded.

Recently, I have also been stuck in this cycle of trying to build useful things and constantly feeling the need to be productive. But after seeing that image, I realized it had been a long time since I made something purely for fun.

So I sat down and drew it.

The drawing turned out terrible, but honestly, I felt relieved. It felt good finally turning something from my imagination into something tangible.

But then the guilt kicked in.

A voice in my head started whispering:
"You just spent an entire hour drawing some random garbage on paper. You could have done something useful instead."

And that thought genuinely bothered me.

Why does spending time on something fun suddenly feel irresponsible?

Is it hustle culture?
Is it social pressure?
Is it the internet constantly making us feel like we should be optimizing every second of our lives?

Where do we even draw the line?

## The Conclusion

I do not really have a concrete answer to any of this.

I do not think this is a black-and-white issue either. Ambition is important. Making money is important. Building things is important.

Most of us spend our lives working so we can support ourselves and the people we care about.

But while thinking about all this, I realized something.

Somewhere along the way, it feels like many of us started treating money, status, and material things as the end goal. But maybe it should be the other way around.

Maybe we should make money so we can spend more time doing things we love with people we love.

Maybe the entire point of all this is to create memories, experiences, and moments that actually mean something to us.

So if there is something you genuinely want to do, and you know it will make you happy, do it.

We are probably going to end up where we end up anyway.

Might as well enjoy the process while getting there.
